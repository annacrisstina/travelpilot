# Local Etiquette

## Greetings

- Greeting norms vary widely: handshakes are standard in most of Europe and North America; bows in Japan and Korea (a nod suffices from foreigners); cheek kisses among acquaintances in France, Spain, and much of Southern Europe (not expected from tourists).
- Verbal greetings matter: in France, saying "Bonjour" when entering a shop is a strong social expectation; in Japan, staff greetings do not require a response.
- Use titles and surnames in formal contexts; first names on introduction are normal in the US, UK, and Netherlands but less so in East Asia.
- Learn hello, please, thank you, and goodbye in the local language; the effort is noticed and appreciated everywhere.
- In the Gulf states, greetings between men and women may not include a handshake unless the woman extends her hand first.

## Dining Etiquette

- **Meal times differ**: Spain dines from 21:00, Italy from 19:30–20:00, while northern Europe, the US, and East Asia eat earlier.
- **Japan**: do not stick chopsticks upright in rice or pass food chopstick-to-chopstick; slurping noodles is acceptable; pour drinks for others rather than yourself.
- **Korea**: elders eat first; receive drinks with two hands; do not lift rice bowls as in Japan or China.
- **Europe**: keep hands visible on the table (not in the lap) in France; bread is placed on the table or side plate; splitting bills item-by-item is uncommon in Southern Europe.
- **Middle East**: eat with the right hand in traditional settings; accepting coffee or tea is a courtesy.
- Finishing food signals appreciation in most Western countries; in some Asian cultures, leaving a small amount signals sufficiency.
- In much of Europe, waitstaff will not bring the bill until asked; this is attentiveness to lingering, not neglect.

## Tipping

- **United States**: tipping is effectively obligatory — 18–20% in restaurants, 1–2 USD per drink, 15–20% for taxis; service workers depend on tips.
- **Western Europe**: service is typically included; rounding up or 5–10% for good service is polite but optional (UK: 10–12.5% where no service charge is added).
- **Japan and South Korea**: tipping is not practiced and can cause confusion or polite refusal.
- **United Arab Emirates**: around 10% is customary in restaurants; many venues add a service charge.
- Check bills for included service charges before adding a tip anywhere.
- Tip in local currency, in cash where card systems do not support it.

## Dress Code

- Religious sites across Europe, the Middle East, and Asia commonly require covered shoulders and knees; carry a light scarf or layer for spontaneous visits.
- In the UAE and other Gulf states, modest dress is expected in malls, souks, and public areas; swimwear belongs only at pools and beaches.
- Southern European cities fine beachwear worn in town in some resorts; urban dress skews neater than in North America.
- Japan and Korea dress relatively conservatively; revealing tops attract more attention than short skirts or shorts.
- Remove shoes when entering homes, temples, traditional restaurants, and guesthouses in Japan, Korea, and much of Asia; slip-on shoes simplify this.
- Practical rule: observe what locals wear and err toward the modest side at religious or formal venues.

## Cultural Respect

- Ask permission before photographing people; photographing women in conservative regions, and certain government or religious buildings, may be prohibited (notably in the UAE).
- Observe religious practice respectfully: keep voices low in places of worship, follow gender-separation rules where they exist, and avoid visits during services unless welcomed.
- During Ramadan in Muslim countries, refrain from eating, drinking, and smoking in public during daylight hours.
- Public displays of affection are viewed differently worldwide: normal in Western Europe, discreet in East Asia, and legally restricted in the Gulf.
- Avoid casually criticizing local politics, religion, or historical grievances; sensitive topics vary by country (e.g., monarchy in Thailand, historical disputes in East Asia).
- Learn locally significant gestures to avoid: thumbs-up and the "OK" sign are offensive in some countries, and pointing with a finger is rude in much of Asia.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Speaking loudly in public spaces, particularly on trains in Japan and Switzerland, where quiet is the norm.
- Assuming English everywhere: opening interactions in the local language, even minimally, changes their tone.
- Standing on the walking side of escalators (conventions differ: right in most of Europe, left in Tokyo).
- Eating while walking in Japan, or in formal Southern European settings.
- Blocking bike lanes in the Netherlands and Denmark; cyclists have priority and expect clear paths.
- Treating haggling as universal: it is expected in Middle Eastern souks and many Asian markets but inappropriate in fixed-price shops worldwide.
- Ignoring queue culture in the UK and Japan, where cutting in line is a serious breach.
- Applying home tipping habits abroad — over-tipping in Japan and under-tipping in the US both cause friction.
